Water safety
  • Filters, purifiers and chemical treatments differ. A filter removes bacteria and protozoa but usually not viruses; a purifier (or filter plus chemical step) is meant to cover viruses too. Check what the maker claims for the specific model.
  • None of these remove chemical contamination (fuel, industrial runoff, many floodwater hazards). If you suspect chemicals, don't drink it; find another source.
  • Boiling is the fallback in public health guidance: a rolling boil for 1 minute (3 minutes at high elevation), per the CDC. If your utility issues a boil-water advisory, follow it.
  • Follow the label on any tablet or drop. Dose, wait time and cold-water adjustments matter. Ask a doctor if you're pregnant, have thyroid issues, or are treating water for infants.

Ready.gov's guidance is about one gallon of water per person per day, for drinking and sanitation, for several days. That is a starting point, not a maximum: heat, illness, pregnancy and nursing all raise needs. Use the calculator on this page to size a supply, then compare filters, purifiers and food options. A filter is not a virus purifier unless the maker says so.

Water storage calculator

Ready.gov's guidance is about one gallon per person per day, for drinking and sanitation, for several days. Pet figures below are typical guidance, not an official rule: roughly half a gallon to a gallon a day for a dog, about a quarter gallon for a cat. Hot weather, illness, pregnancy and nursing all raise needs.

Water weighs about 8.3 pounds per gallon, so plan floor strength and how you'll carry it. Store in food-grade containers away from sunlight and fuels, and replace stored water on the schedule the container or your health department recommends. Sources: Ready.gov, CDC.

Common questions

How much water should I store per person per day?

Ready.gov's guidance is about one gallon per person per day, for drinking and sanitation, for several days. That is a starting point, not a cap: heat, illness, pregnancy and nursing all raise needs. Use the calculator on this page to size a supply.

How much water should I store for pets?

There is no single official pet figure on Ready.gov's water page. The calculator on this page uses typical guidance, not an official rule: roughly half a gallon to a gallon a day for a dog, and about a quarter gallon for a cat. Adjust for size, heat and medical needs.

How much does stored water weigh?

About 8.3 pounds per gallon, so a three-day supply for one person is already about 25 pounds before you add food or gear. Plan where you will store it and how you would carry it.

What is the difference between a filter and a purifier?

A filter is generally meant to remove bacteria and protozoa, not viruses. A purifier, or a filter plus a chemical step, is meant to cover viruses too. Check the maker's claim for the exact model. None of the treatments on this chart remove chemical contamination such as fuel or many floodwater hazards.

Should I boil water if I am unsure?

Boiling is the fallback in public health guidance. The CDC's advice, already noted on this page, is a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes at high elevation. If your utility issues a boil-water advisory, follow that first.

How long does emergency food last?

Shelf life depends on the food and the storage conditions; heat shortens it. On the chart below, some freeze-dried pantry foods carry maker claims of up to 25 or 30 years. USDA figures for canned goods are much shorter: high-acid foods roughly 12–18 months, low-acid foods roughly 2–5 years. Some backpacking meals on the chart have no confirmed shelf-life claim in our notes, so those cells stay blank. Rotate what you store; the shelf-life tracker can help.

How many calories should I plan per person per day?

The food chart uses about 2,000 kcal per adult as a common planning figure. That is a planning aid, not a medical recommendation — check your own needs, especially for children, pregnancy or hard work in the heat or cold.
