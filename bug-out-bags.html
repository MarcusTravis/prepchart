Bug-Out Bag Lists Compared

What the official and well-known emergency lists actually recommend, summarized in our own words with links to the originals. Pick the focus that matches your situation: leaving in a hurry, sheltering at home, or getting home on foot.

Compiled: Nov 19, 2026 · prices blank until verified · What changed

A 72-hour or go-bag kit is a set of supplies to get you through a few days if you have to leave or if services stop. Official lists from Ready.gov, the Red Cross and others usually agree on water, food, light, a radio, first aid, medications, hygiene, documents, cash and a way to charge a phone. They differ on whether the kit is meant to stay at home or be carried out the door. Item counts below are counts of printed recommendations, not a packed bag.

How to read this

Item counts are counts of the recommendations a list prints, not the contents of a packed bag. Some bullets bundle several items, so counts between lists are not directly comparable. Weight and cost stay blank unless a publisher states a figure; for other rough weights, try the bag builder (editorial estimates, labeled).

A "bug-out bag" and a "home kit" are not the same thing. Many official lists are written for a kit you keep at home and can grab if you have to leave. A pack meant for walking out needs the same categories at a weight you can carry.

Where they agree, and where they don't

Almost every list starts in the same place: water and food for a few days, light, a way to hear official updates, first aid, medications, hygiene, copies of documents, some cash, and a way to charge a phone. Most also mention pets, children, or medical needs if those apply to you.

They diverge on what the kit is for. Ready.gov, the Red Cross, the Salvation Army, Texas Ready and the CDC write mainly for a household kit you keep at home and can grab if you leave — often including utility shutoff tools or plastic sheeting. NYC Emergency Management, Washington's grab-and-go kit, and CAL FIRE's wildfire go bag are more explicitly packs for leaving in a hurry. Canada's list uses two litres of water per person per day, not a gallon, and splits a basic kit from lighter grab-and-go bags. The UK Be Ready checklist is written mainly as three days of supplies at home; a grab bag is mentioned only in passing. The Australian Red Cross adds a separate recovery kit of document copies and sentimental items. The Ten Essentials remain a hiking and self-rescue systems list, and The Prepared is a carry-out packing list with a stated weight budget.

Source lists belong to their publishers; we link out and don't reproduce them. Details and check dates are in SOURCES.md.

Common questions

What typically goes in a 72-hour kit?

Across Ready.gov, the Red Cross and the other official lists on this page, the shared core is water, food, light, a radio or other way to get official updates, first aid, medications, hygiene, copies of documents, some cash and a way to charge a phone. Many lists also mention supplies for pets, children or medical needs if those apply to you.

How is a bug-out bag different from a home kit?

A home kit is meant to be kept in the house and used if you shelter in place or grab it as you leave. A bug-out or go bag is packed to be carried. Ready.gov and the Red Cross write mainly for a household kit. NYC Emergency Management, Washington's grab-and-go kit and CAL FIRE's wildfire go bag are more explicitly packs for leaving in a hurry.

How heavy should a bug-out bag be?

None of the official lists in this comparison states a packed weight, and we leave that cell blank unless a publisher gives a figure. If you have to walk, it has to be a weight you can carry. The bag builder uses editorial estimates. The Prepared's Level 1 list on this page is stated as under 20 pounds.

Why do the item counts differ from list to list?

Counts are counts of printed recommendations, not the contents of a packed bag. Some bullets bundle several items, so one list's “14” is not directly comparable to another's “27.” Read the notes under each count and open the original list.

Do these lists include a weight or a cost?

Usually not. We leave weight and cost blank unless the publisher states a figure. We don't invent one. For rough editorial weights, try the bag builder.

Should each person have their own bag?

NYC Emergency Management says everyone in a household should have a Go Bag that is sturdy and easy to carry. Canada's GetPrepared guidance also suggests a separate grab-and-go bag for each household member if you need to evacuate. Customize it for the person who will carry it.

Where should I start if I am new to this?

Skim this comparison to see where official lists agree, then use the bag builder for a checklist and the water and food page to size water and calories. If you are planning for a specific hazard, see the scenario guides.
